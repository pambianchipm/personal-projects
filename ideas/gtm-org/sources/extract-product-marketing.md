## Section 1: Crafting Positioning Concepts (pp.4–19)

### Where concepts sit in the research process (p.5)
"Reminder: you're using these in customer research." Four-stage pipeline:

1. **Customer exploratory** — Interviews; Surveys; Test pitches
2. **Market + competitive insights** — Size and trends; Gap analysis; Competitor brand + product assessments; Competitor mapping
3. **Craft concepts** — "3-5 different ways to position the product"; Feature lists; "Short narrative (e.g. paragraph) form"
4. **Test/validate** — "Test concepts and feature lists with target audience"; "Refine/combine and test again"; "Review final results, choose"

**When?** "Typically when you're ready to do formal positioning work (hire outside help). Could also do earlier, scrappy research using one concept to get reactions to a product idea (instead of to test positioning alternatives)."

### What concepts are / aren't (p.6)
- **Positioning concepts ARE:** "Designed for research"; "Focused on uncovering insight"; "Brief (so you can show multiple)"; "'Marketing-copy like'"
- **Positioning concepts AREN'T:** "Actual marketing copy"; "Deeply technical"; "Sell or spec sheets"; "Advertisements"

### Brand or product positioning? (p.7)
Q: "Are they for brand or product positioning?" A: **"Yes"** (i.e., both).

### The basic concept template (p.8; alt formatting on p.47)
A plain one-page card. Layout, verbatim structure:

```
[code letter, top-right corner, e.g. "M"]

Lorem ipsum introductory sentence that introduces the context or problem.

[Pretend Name] is the lorem ipsum introduction to your core benefit. It lorem
ipsum key selling point, supported by feature or fact proof. It lorem ipsum second
key selling point, with feature, feature feature. And, it has feature, feature, so that
you can lorem ipsum key selling point three.

[Pretend Name] - lorem ipsum endline that reiterates the higher order benefit
and/or the top key selling point.

              1     2     3     4     5
```

Element-by-element rules:

- **Font (p.9):** "Pick a bland, easy to read font. You don't want people reacting to liking/disliking the font. Arial is a good choice."
- **Code letters (p.10, repeated p.47):** "You'll likely test least three territories (e.g. different north star options … core ideas like simplicity, innovation, achievement). Each of the positioning territories you're testing gets a code letter. That letter stays consistent through copy rounds so that you can aggregate scores at the end. Pick them randomly, skip those that correspond with school letter grades to avoid any unconscious bias." (So no A, B, C, D, F.)
- **1–5 scale printed on the page (p.11):** "Each concept also has a 1-5 scale on the bottom. This is so that you can easily capture ratings as you review each concept live with your research participants. Online research tools will let them pick digitally, but I usually put them on the page in case of in-person conversations or simply for ease of instruction over video meeting."
- **First line = context/problem (p.12):** "In your first line, you introduce the context or high level problem the customer faces. This is to test whether or not the core issue you think you're addressing is the correct one. It also allows you to test how much 'let me tell you what your problem is' you can do with your audience (sometimes it's great, sometimes they hate it). If you find in the first few interviews that people hate it, just delete it."
- **Fictional name (p.13):** "Often you'll come up with a fictional name for your product or brand. This is for confidentiality and because people can get very hung up on names ('I scored it a 1 because I didn't like the name'). Researching naming is not the point here, so you give it a fictional name (and tell them it's fictional) so they can mostly ignore it. The exception: if you're conducting the research yourself and they know where you work, you can't invent a brand (you can still make up a product name)."
- **Core promise (p.14):** "Next you share the core promise that your brand or product offers to the customer. This is some version of the one liner from your positioning framework."
- **KSPs with proof (p.15):** "Then, you articulate your key selling points, supported with a relevant proof point or feature (or two) for each. Usually you can get to ~3 KSPs in a single concept. Note that you'll want to prioritize the elements of your positioning framework that are the most linked to the core promise - so, there will be some differences in what you talk about in each concept. You'll just have to look out for when a concept gets a high score because of a hit feature, not because the main idea was landing."
- **Endline (p.16):** "Wrap it up with something pithy. Two choices. One: restate the core promise, maybe with a KSP attached. Two: test a creative endline. Pick #1 if you're having trouble articulating the core benefit and need to try different ways. Pick #2 if what you need is feedback on messaging you might like to use in the real world." (Example endline from a prior session's brand case study — an empowerment concept: **"because when we each do a little, it adds up to a lot."**)

### How the qual research works (p.20) — "a quick snapshot"
Three interview phases:

1. **Grounding** — "Give ground rules (confidential, asking for honest opinions, etc). Get to know the interviewee (role, view on the market). Use a few minutes to explore needs and/or competition depending on where you have knowledge gaps."
2. **Concept Review** — "Read the concept out loud, and ask respondent to: Write main idea on the top / Circle likes / Cross out dislikes / Underline anything confusing / Give it a score. Then discuss. After all of them, rank and explore why."
3. **Feature List Review** — "If you have these to test, share in batches of 3-5 (label and brief description for each). Score on importance and on uniqueness. It works best to give them a table to fill in. Then at the end, pick top 3 and discuss why."

**Opinionated rule (p.20):** "Conducting the interviews is an ideal use of outside consulting support. It's hard to manage your own listening bias, and also you'll get much more honest feedback if the interviewer is a neutral party and reports responses anonymously."

## Section 2: Defining Your Key Selling Points (KSPs) (pp.21–29)

Framing (p.21): "A key ingredient in your concepts? Key selling points. Let's talk about how to figure out what yours should be."

### Step 1 — Brainstorm the laundry list (p.22)
"Getting started: use your existing insights, brainstorm, and then organize."

1. "Mine your customer and market insights for product benefits that match needs + differentiate you. Write down bullets."
2. "Generate an additional list of why to buy bullets based on what you want your product narrative to be."
3. "Use those two inputs to create a laundry list."

**Rule:** "Keep them short (under 10 words)."

### Brainstorm prompts (p.23) — verbatim
"Here are some prompts to get you started. A great way, if you have some co-founders, employees or advisors, is to invite them to capture ideas (separately) and then share."

- "Tell me in three bullets why someone should buy your product."
- "What is your special sauce? The thing you do that's unique, or better than what others do?"
- "Fast forward a few years. You have a customer who is telling a peer why they chose your product. What things do they list?"
- "What things are a competitive advantage for you now that will still be an advantage in 5, 10 years?"
- "What things are true about your product or service philosophy that you intend to retain no matter what happens with the product suite and company path?"

### Live practice exercises (pp.24–25)
- **Practice 1 (p.24):** "Open up a doc or grab some paper. What is your special sauce? The thing you do that's unique, or better than what others do? Write down at least two things."
- **Practice 2 (p.25):** "Fast forward a few years. You have a customer who is telling a peer why they chose your product. What things do they list? Write down at least three things. Now drop your list into the chat."

### Step 2 — Cluster and narrow (p.26)
"After this step, you'll have a big, messy list. Next up: cluster (you'll have duplicates, or things that are very similar), then narrow to a list of 7-10 candidates."

### Step 3 — Narrow 7–10 to final 3–5 KSPs (p.27)
"Typically you'll use one (or more) techniques to get to a final 3-5 KSPs":

1. "Explore your 7-10 options via concept research, then pick the winners"
2. "Try to fill in a positioning framework column for every one (usually you find some just don't work or feel quite right)"
3. "Vote or prioritize and decide."

"In these scenarios, you use the concept research to validate that you narrowed correctly and to identify the best language to use to express the KSPs."

### The positioning framework (visible in p.28's example graphic) — row structure
| Row | Definition (verbatim from slide) |
|---|---|
| **Frame of ref** | "The category where your brand operates (e.g. what kind of company are you)" |
| **Higher order benefit** | "One core idea, the main thing you want to stand for" |
| **Product promise** | "One sentence that captures your core product promise to customers" |
| **Key selling points** | Four "Benefit" boxes (each benefit + supporting detail) |
| **(temp) Headline** | "A creative expression of the main idea/core promise" |

### KSP ordering rule (p.28)
"Suggestion: arrange your KSPs in order of importance (e.g. in US, most important is on the left). Usually this is a rough approximation of airtime or priority in marketing assets."

## Section 3: Objection Handling (pp.30–34)

### Definition and timing (p.31)
"Last session we introduced objection handling. This is a product marketing task where you go outside in and give your own product a critical eye. Often companies don't do this until they're prepping for press. **I recommend you do it starting when your product is still a loose concept. Why? You find the holes in your product/market fit.**"

### Practice exercise — roleplay three critics (p.32)
Participants generate objections from three personas:

1. "You're a competitor's sales person."
2. "You're a member of the press, known for tough reporting (and being a bit salty)."
3. "You're a customer with a tendency to be very skeptical."

Share-out (p.33): "What's one thing that you captured? What might you do about that info now? Hand raise or drop into chat."

### The Assess → Answer → Address process (p.34)
1. **Assess** — "Which of the objections are most worrisome? The biggest risks/why?"
2. **Answer** — "Craft an internal and a press-facing answer for each. How satisfactory are they? How comfortable are you? Now what?"
3. **Address** — "When you find a worrisome objection that doesn't really have a good answer, go back to the product. Can you fix it before it hits the market?"

## Section 4: Bonus — Competition & Battle Cards (pp.35–42)

### Three-step process (p.36)
"Recommend a three step process":
1. "Mine the customer and market research"
2. "Identify top alternatives/competitors"
3. "Build your story to sell against them" — "We will focus most of our time here."

### Why competitive framing matters (p.37) — verbatim
"One of the things we discussed last time is that product positioning does not happen in a vacuum. We need to move from the early stage of innovation centered on 'this technology is amazing' to commercially relevant framing – to **'this technology is amazing, this product meets your needs, and here's why you should choose it over anything else.'**"

### Step one: identify the alternatives — three competitor types (p.38)
1. **Inaction** — "Sometimes the biggest competitor is inertia. The most likely alternative? Do nothing."
2. **Build** — "Often an alternative is to build a product internally. One component of your value prop may be why it's better to buy (or license) than create from scratch."
3. **Buy** — "This is the traditional definition of competition. Other products, made by other companies, that the buyer may choose instead."

### Step two: prioritize and assess top alternatives (p.39)
1. **Prioritize** — "Start with 3-5 top alternatives. These are the ones on the buyer's 'day one' list."
2. **Investigate** — "SWOT for each / What are the KSPs? / Primary features? / What's great about X? / Who should pick it"
3. **Summarize** — "Put together a dossier on each key alternative that summarizes what you know about them."

### Making competitive selling practical (p.40)
"The key? **Know your stuff.** Where you win, where they're better. And have very specific and practical ways that you discuss the trade offs and why you're the right choice. A key tool: battle cards."

### Battle card definition (p.41)
"A battle card is an internally facing sales enablement asset. Its purpose is to help a person selling your product know how to sell against a specific alternative."

### Battle card template (p.42) — complete structure
One card per competitor:

- **Logo** (competitor's)
- **Overview** — "[summary of the competitor and their offering]"
- **Key stats** — Location: / # of employees: / Regions served: / Key customers: / Funding:
- **Comparison** — table with columns **Competitor | Us**, rows: Price / [other factor] / [feature/capability] / [feature/capability] / [feature/capability]
- **Why we win** — Reason one / Reason two / Reason three
- **Why they win** — Reason one / Reason two / Reason three
- **Resources** — "[links to documents and reports with more info]"
- **Landmines** — "[set of questions for a prospect designed to highlight downsides or risks of going with the competitor]" — Question one / Question two / Question three

## The 8 most load-bearing ideas for an early-stage startup's product marketing

1. **You are not your customer — never skip primary research**; positioning choices get validated with real target-audience interviews, not founder intuition (pp.5, 20, 44).
2. **Test positioning as 3–5 brief, "marketing-copy like" concepts** — one page each, bland font, random code letter, 1–5 scale, fictional product name — so respondents react to the idea, not the artifact (pp.5–13).
3. **Every concept follows the same skeleton: context/problem line → core promise (your positioning one-liner) → ~3 KSPs each backed by a proof point or feature → pithy endline** — and watch for concepts scoring high on a "hit feature" rather than the main idea (pp.8, 12–16, 18).
4. **KSPs come from a disciplined funnel: brainstorm a laundry list (bullets under 10 words, ideas captured separately by founders/advisors) → cluster → 7–10 candidates → concept-test/framework-fit/vote down to a final 3–5, ordered by importance** since order approximates marketing airtime (pp.22–28).
5. **Anchor everything in a positioning framework: frame of reference (what kind of company you are), higher order benefit (one core idea), product promise (one sentence), KSPs, headline** — the Gemini example shows what breaks when the frame of reference or single core idea is missing (pp.14–15, 18, 28).
6. **Do objection handling while the product is still a loose concept, not at press prep** — roleplay a competitor's salesperson, a salty journalist, and a skeptical customer, then Assess/Answer/Address, and when an objection has no good answer, fix the product before it ships (pp.31–34, 44).
7. **Your competitors include "do nothing" (inertia) and "build it internally," not just other vendors** — prioritize the 3–5 alternatives on the buyer's "day one" list and build a dossier (SWOT, KSPs, features, who should pick it) for each (pp.38–39, 44).
8. **Move from "this technology is amazing" to commercially relevant framing** — "this product meets your needs, and here's why you should choose it over anything else" — operationalized in battle cards with why-we-win/why-they-win, a comparison table, and "landmine" questions for prospects (pp.37, 40–42).
